Doodle Duel.
Draw. It guesses on every stroke.

A shad0w image table reads your drawing as pixels and names it in microseconds, right in this tab: no neural network, no server, no GPU. It learned from … doodles people drew for Google's Quick, Draw! game, finished and half-drawn, and it says "I know!" only when its answer is certified.

Or just draw any of the words below.

how

Pixels to items to a certified answer.

The same idea shad0w uses for text, applied to images. Your drawing is rendered to a small picture; 256 "ferns" compare pairs of nearby pixels, a coarse grid records where the ink is, and a perceptual hash catches near-duplicates. Those become item numbers, exactly like words in a sentence, and the table adds up what each item says about each word. No convolution, no model download beyond the table itself.

  • Teacher. Here, the word each player was asked to draw (the game's label). In your app the teacher is your vision model: shad0w learns its answers, and the certificate is against that model.
  • Certified means confident enough to pass a test set in advance: on certified answers it disagrees with the teacher at most 5% of the time (90% confidence).
  • Where it works. Drawings, icons, product photos, screens of a known app: images where the layout of light and dark tells the class. On natural photos (CIFAR-10) the pixel table certifies nothing and defers everything; there, an optional CLIP tier certified 73% at ~28 ms instead of a vision model's 2.6 s.
  • Data. Google's Quick, Draw! dataset (CC BY 4.0), the drawings the game itself recognised.
