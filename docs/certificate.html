The certificate, explained#

In plain words#

You give shad0w a few thousand answers your model already made. shad0w trains the table on most of them and keeps the rest aside. On those held-out answers it asks one question: if the table only answers when its confidence is above some threshold t, how often does it disagree with your model?

It tries thresholds from strict to lenient. It accepts a threshold only if the disagreement is low enough that, even allowing for bad luck in which answers were held out, the true rate is still at most α. It stops at the first threshold that fails. The last accepted threshold is the certificate.

The result is one sentence you can show a reviewer:

On traffic like the calibration slice, among answers the table serves itself, it disagrees with your model at most α of the time. This statement fails with probability at most δ.

Three things this is not:

  • It is not a bound against the truth. It compares the table with your model, so if your model is wrong, the table is certified to be wrong in the same way. For a bound against the truth, calibrate with real labels (shad0w calibrate, about 300 per question).
  • It is not valid under arbitrary traffic shifts. It holds for traffic drawn like the calibration slice. That is why there is a drift guard, live audits and shad0w certify.
  • It is not per-request. It is a rate over served answers, not a promise about any single answer.

Formally#

Setting. The calibration slice has n texts that were never used for fitting, with your model's answers tᵢ. The table outputs a prediction ŷᵢ and a confidence cᵢ. Disagreement is eᵢ = 1[ŷᵢ ≠ tᵢ].

Procedure (shad0w.reliability.SelectiveRiskController):

  1. Sort calibration points by confidence, descending.
  2. Fix a grid of 50 accepted-set sizes k, linearly spaced from k₀ to n. The start k₀ depends only on (n, α, δ), never on the data: k₀ = max( ⌈log δ / log(1 − α)⌉, ⌈0.05 n⌉, min(50, n), k_tol ). Here k_tol is the smallest k at which ⌊αk/2⌋ disagreements still certify. Starting there means one unlucky disagreement among the very top answers cannot end the test.
  3. For each k, from strict to lenient, compute the Clopper–Pearson upper bound on the disagreement rate among the top-k: U_k = BetaInv(1 − δ; E_k + 1, k − E_k), where E_k counts disagreements in the top k. Stop at the first k with U_k > α.
  4. The certified threshold τ is the confidence of the last accepted k. If none was accepted, τ = ∞ and nothing is served.

Guarantee. Fixed-sequence testing of a pre-ordered family of hypotheses controls the family-wise error at δ, with no multiplicity penalty (Learn-then-Test, Angelopoulos et al. 2021). With probability ≥ 1 − δ over the calibration sample:

P( ŷ(x) ≠ teacher(x) | c(x) ≥ τ*, x ~ D_cal ) ≤ α

Assumptions. Calibration and future inputs are exchangeable, meaning drawn from the same distribution D_cal. The bound is marginal over D_cal: it is not conditional on class, and a shift in the class mix can break it.

Against the truth. If your model is right with probability q on the served inputs, the served error against the truth is at most α + (1 − q), by a union bound. In tests, cascade accuracy tracked the teacher's to within a point.

Does it actually hold? We checked#

The repository has a simulation test (tests/test_certificate_validity.py):

  • It draws 300 independent calibration sets from a known population.
  • It fits the certificate on each one.
  • It measures the true disagreement rate at each certified threshold, using a 1,000,000-sample population.
  • It asserts that the fraction of draws where the truth exceeds α is at most δ.

The same check with 400 draws per setting, during development (population A of the test: disagreement likelier at low confidence):

α Calibration size Draws where the bound failed Promise (δ) Mean offload
2% 3,000 5.0% ≤ 10% 32%
5% 900 7.2% ≤ 10% 65%
5% 3,000 5.0% ≤ 10% 73%

Changing where the sequence starts (k_tol above) cut the share of runs that certified nothing from 15% to 3% (α = 2%, n = 3,000). The failure rate stayed within δ.

On real data, realised disagreement on held-out test sets at α = 5% was 3.9–5.7% (mean of three seeds) across seven intent and topic tasks. The worst single seed reached 6.5% on a 600-item test set, which is within what δ = 0.1 and test-set noise allow. On a test slice dominated by one class, it reached 6–7%. That is the class-mix shift the assumptions warn about. Every run is in the benchmarks.

Drift guard and audits#

  • Drift guard (no labels). At calibration time, shad0w picks the confidence level t whose below-t mass equals the observed disagreement rate (ATC, Garg et al. 2022). In production it tracks the share of answers below t over a 500-answer window, and raises drift when that share exceeds the calibration estimate by 3 points. Certified answers are then deferred. It catches shifts that lower confidence, such as a surge of out-of-scope traffic. It cannot see shifts where the table stays confidently wrong.
  • Spot-check audits (the Shadow wrapper). A random audit_rate share of certified answers is also sent to your model. stats() reports the observed disagreement and its Clopper–Pearson upper bound.
  • Anytime-valid auditor (shad0w.audit.Auditor). It consumes spot-check labels and keeps a confidence sequence that is valid under continuous monitoring, using betting plus active prediction-powered estimates. It tightens the threshold when the bound exceeds α. In stream tests, about 0.7% of traffic as audit labels kept the served error within α under prevalence and prior shifts.

Why not certify on pseudo-labels or synthetic data?#

A bound is only valid against the labels used to compute it. Certificates fitted on a compile-time encoder's pseudo-labels or on synthetic data were invalid in our tests: 14.6% and 19.3% realised error at a 5% target. So label-free bundles are marked uncalibrated and serve nothing until real labels or teacher answers certify them.
